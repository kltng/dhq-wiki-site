# DHQ in the Public Eye

**Authors:** [[Melissa_Terras]]

**Volume 001, Issue 2** (2007-09-12) | editorial | 866 words

**Keywords:** [[collaboration-interdisciplinarity]], [[collaboration-interdisciplinarity]], [[media-studies-dhq]], [[publishing-dhq]], [[archives-access]], [[digital-humanities-dhq]]


Welcome to the second issue of DHQ. It is difficult, at this stage in a journal’s
            progress, to monitor uptake, success, or readership. Where can we start? The launch of
            DHQ was emblazoned across various discussion lists (such as [Humanist](http://www.princeton.edu/humanist/)[Digital Classicist](http://wiki.digitalclassicist.org/discussion)[Digital Medievalist](http://listserv.uleth.ca/mailman/listinfo/dm-l)[TEI-L](http://listserv.brown.edu/archives/cgi-bin/wa?a0=tei-l)[Grand
                Text Auto](http://grandtextauto.gatech.edu/)[Stoa](http://www.stoa.org/)[Wikipedia](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/digital_humanities)[40 different home pages](http://www.google.co.uk/search?hl=en&q=link%3ahttp%3a%2f%2fwww.digitalhumanities.org%2fdhq%2f&btng=search&meta=)[del.icio.us](http://del.icio.us/url/723fc5626023597778345bc4297699a6)


Still, with the comments feature yet to be enabled (we are still building the back end
            of the site, and this experiment of a journal will continue to evolve with each issue)
            it is difficult to gain a feel for opinions regarding the journal — save from those
            individuals which have taken the time to email the editors directly with their
            congratulations. Comments on del.icio.us generally raised one question about our format:
            [Where is the RSS
                feed?](http://del.icio.us/url/723fc5626023597778345bc4297699a6)


So far so good, even if the digital humanities community can be viewed as being fairly
            small, fairly insular, and fairly unlikely to reach out to wider academic and
            technological sphere. We at DHQ hope that we will eventually reach a wider audience (and
            trust our readers will help us do so), introducing the type and range of activities the
            digital humanities community is interested in, and featuring energetic, novel, and
            interesting articles on a variety of research, making use of all the Internet
            technologies at our disposal.


One of the papers in this, our second issue, has already done just that. Dennis G. Jerz's
            [Somewhere Nearby is Colossal Cave: Examining Will
                Crowther's Original Adventure in Code and in Kentucky](/dhq/vol/1/2/000009/000009.html)[rec.arts.int-fiction](http://groups.google.com/group/rec.arts.int-fiction/browse_frm/thread/607acaf1a279d4dd/bd53b672a185d177#)HOLY MOLY!It
                    is clear on a single reading that this is the most important single paper ever
                    written on the history of interactive fiction[Boing
                Boing](http://www.boingboing.net/2007/08/13/maze_of_twisty_littl.html)directory of wonderful things[Slashdot](http://games.slashdot.org/article.pl?sid=07/08/14/011230)slashdotted[Metafilter](http://www.metafilter.com/63774/say-xyzzynothing-happens)What academic research should aspire to beI can feel a new LOLCATS
                meme coming on. (I can haz mint-cake?)


In addition, publication on DHQ has made the original game available again for a new
            audience. When the preprint version of this article became available on the internet in
            August 2007, [Matthew
                Russoto](http://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/games/source/adv_crowther.zip)[David
                Kinder](http://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/games/pc/adv_crowther_win.zip)


Of course, this is due to the contents of the article: Will Crowther's classic computer
            game **Colossal Cave Adventure****Digital Humanities Quarterly**


We hope other articles in this issue tickle readers in the same way. Some useful
            connections emerge between Jerz's article and Eric Eve's analysis of the challenges of
            interactive fiction design, both of which present a thematic perspective grounded in a
            detailed view of the structure and coding of the game. Steve Anderson examines the
            emerging digital practices and techniques that distinguish the digital avant garde, and
            David Hoover argues for the critical and interpretive value of text analysis.


We also hope that the success of this article indicates the importance and benefits of
            publishing in DHQ. We encourage you to continue with us in this experiment: submit,
            review, and enjoy.
