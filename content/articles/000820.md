### Curation as Scholarly Method


Digital scholarly projects have increasingly come to occupy the space between
                    research, infrastructure, and public engagement. They take the form of annotated
                    corpora, multimedia archives, interactive platforms, and metadata-rich
                    collections, each demanding modes of expertise that exceed conventional
                    disciplinary boundaries. Their value lies not only in what they present but in
                    how they are structured: in their architecture, their accessibility, their
                    iterative design, and their responsiveness to multiple publics. Yet these forms
                    of labour remain institutionally ambiguous, largely because they fail to
                    resemble the scholarly genres against which academic rigour is measured. This
                    paper takes up one specific instance of digital scholarly work — curation — as a
                    site through which to explore this misrecognition. Curation, as used here,
                    refers not simply to the management or digitisation of materials, but to the
                    interpretive, infrastructural, and methodological labour involved in rendering
                    knowledge accessible, legible, and durable. Through the examination of
                    university-based curatorial projects in India, this section illustrates how
                    curation performs the core functions of scholarship, selection,
                    contextualisation, annotation, and theorisation, yet remains peripheral to
                    academic evaluation.[^: The National Institutional Ranking Framework (NIRF)

The misrecognition of labour in the Indian university emerges from an evaluative
                    ecosystem that continues to define scholarship through a narrow genealogy of
                    outputs that are textual, authored, bounded, and citation ready. The University
                    Grants Commission’s Academic Performance Index (API), for instance, lists
                    monographs, journal articles, and externally funded projects as primary
                    indicators of scholarly productivity. There is no category for collaborative
                    metadata architecture, no provision for the intellectual design of repositories,
                    no scoring mechanism for the creation of vernacular corpora. The university
                    knows how to read the journal article, the citation, the conference paper. It
                    does not yet know how to read the infrastructure that enables those texts to
                    circulate. As a result, digital curators are forced into a kind of rhetorical
                    double labour: building the archive and then narrating its scholarly legitimacy
                    in a vocabulary not built to accommodate.[^: This reflects widespread
                        institutional practice, as docu][^: and  argue

Curation, then, is not an isolated exception within the university, but a
                    paradigmatic instance of a broader transformation in the form, labour, and
                    temporality of digital scholarly projects. It exemplifies the convergence of
                    research, design, and infrastructure that now characterises much of contemporary
                    humanities work: platforms that are iterative rather than conclusive, authored
                    by teams rather than individuals, structured around access rather than closure.
                    The dilemmas faced by curators — how to render fragments legible, how to encode
                    context, how to build for users not yet imagined — are the dilemmas of digital
                    scholarship writ large. Whether in the creation of linguistic corpora,
                    multimedia editions, data visualisations, or public history portals, the same
                    questions recur: what counts as scholarly method when knowledge is mediated
                    through interface? What forms of rigour are legible to institutions built on
                    print? This examination of curation offers a lens through which we understand
                    how digital projects demand, and produce, a different conceptual grammar of
                    scholarly value.


### Verification, Value, and Peer Review


If digital curation is to be recognised as a legitimate mode of scholarly labour,
                    it must be supported not only through institutional policies but through the
                    rethinking of the evaluative frameworks that govern academic legitimacy. In
                    India, academic value is tethered to a limited set of outputs, peer-reviewed
                    publications, citation counts, and journal rankings, and it is formalised in
                    structures such as the UGC’s Academic Performance Index (API), NAAC’s
                    accreditation rubrics, and funding protocols issued by state research bodies.
                    These systems remain calibrated to traditional textual scholarship.


To be published, in the academic sphere, is to be sanctioned by a system of
                    editorial and peer oversight whose parameters are historically shaped by
                    print-based models of authorship. Digital projects often fall outside these
                    contours. As such, they challenge the core assumptions upon which scholarly
                    publishing, and the economies it supports, are built. That challenge is
                    precisely why they remain peripheral. Their value is not unrecognised; it is
                    unaccommodated. The problem, then, is not that digital projects are
                    unrecognisable. It is that they are published outside the regimes of value that
                    govern academic credibility.

                    layer of knowledge. Yet institutional frameworks have no grammar to parse this

### Toward a Hybrid Framework for Evaluating Digital Scholarship in India


If the preceding sections have outlined the epistemic and institutional
                    exclusions faced by digital scholarly projects in India, this section turns
                    toward the question of what might be done. Recognition cannot remain rhetorical;
                    it must be encoded in forms that are legible to the systems that govern
                    scholarly value. The challenge is not only to validate digital scholarship, but
                    to develop evaluative models that are methodologically rigorous, contextually
                    grounded, and structurally embedded. Existing frameworks, such as those proposed
                    by the Modern Language Association (MLA) and the American Historical Association
                    (AHA) have laid important groundwork, particularly in recognising multimodal
                    forms and collaborative labour #mla_2024#aha_2015

                    particularly those developed by MLA, AHA, and NEH, but extends them in key

#### 1. Multimodal and Multilingual Sensitivity


While many global DH frameworks recognise multimodality, few grapple with the
                        linguistic infrastructure of multilingual metadata, vernacular scripting,
                        and transliteration. The hybrid model insists that evaluative criteria must
                        extend beyond usability or aesthetics to include linguistic inclusivity,
                        script variation, and the epistemic labour of cross-script metadata
                        creation.


#### 2. Infrastructure as Scholarly Proposition


Existing frameworks often treat infrastructure as a neutral backdrop. This
                        model rejects that separation. Drawing on Stephen Ramsay’s assertion that
                        building can be an argument, it insists that infrastructure, metadata
                        logics, interface architecture, preservation design, is itself a form of
                        scholarly thought #ramsay_2014


#### 3. Ongoing and Iterative Evaluation


Following Kathleen Fitzpatrick’s call for process-aware review, this model
                        emphasises lifecycle-based evaluation #fitzpatrick_2011


#### 4. Attribution and Distributed Authorship


Unlike traditional evaluation models that privilege sole authorship, this
                        framework recognises that digital scholarship is often collaborative and
                        layered. It foregrounds the need to document, credit, and evaluate labour
                        that is infrastructural, affective, or design-based. Building on models like
                        the CRediT taxonomy and adapted for the humanities, the framework insists
                        that project contributors, including metadata specialists, coders, interface
                        designers, and archivists, be formally named and credited. This is
                        particularly urgent in the Indian context, where the labour of project
                        fellows and contract researchers is routinely disaggregated from
                        institutional reward systems.


#### 5. Embedded Institutional Reform


While global evaluative frameworks offer useful models, their institutional
                        assumptions do not always align with the Indian academic context. Here, the
                        challenge is epistemic: Universities continue to privilege abstract,
                        text-bound, and singular authorship models shaped by colonial-modern
                        legacies. A meaningful framework for evaluating digital scholarship in India
                        must draw on local intellectual traditions that interrogate how legitimacy
                        is assigned.


#### 6. Inclusion of Affective and Ethical Labour


Projects that deal with marginalised communities, contested memory, or
                        historical trauma involve significant affective and ethical judgement:
                        decisions about anonymity, interface legibility, and representational
                        justice. This labour is often invisible but epistemically central. The
                        hybrid model proposes that ethical curation, not only what is shown, but how
                        and why, is a reviewable scholarly component, particularly when digital
                        projects work with oral histories, sensitive images, or
                        community-contributed data.


#### 7. Expanded Reviewer Composition


Interdisciplinary output demands interdisciplinary review. The hybrid model
                        calls for peer review bodies that include domain experts, curators,
                        designers, technologists, archivists, and community stakeholders, especially
                        in fields where institutional memory is short and where documentation
                        functions as a proxy for argument.[^: The NEH Office of Digital
                            Humanities (USA) includes ]


#### 8. Processual Paratexts as Scholarship


Version logs, data models, controlled vocabularies, documentation protocols,
                        these are not peripheral materials. They are the argumentative scaffolding
                        of the digital project. The hybrid model insists that these be reviewed,
                        cited, and archived with the same seriousness as introductions or literature
                        reviews.


#### 9. Academic Infrastructure as Public Platform


Finally, the hybrid model recognises that in the Indian context, digital
                        projects often stand in for absent state archives. They are infrastructures
                        of access, repair, and public memory. Their review must reflect this dual
                        status, acknowledging that to build a corpus, translate a script, or sustain
                        an archive is to produce knowledge.

### Conclusion: Towards Recognition


Digital collections are not peripheral to scholarship, they are among its most
                    vital contemporary forms. They materialise scholarly labour in code, metadata,
                    interface, and community practice; they carry argument not only in words but in
                    form and structure. Their marginalisation is not a matter of ambiguity, but of
                    institutional inertia, systems built for a different media ecology, struggling
                    to recognise the contours of digital epistemology. What is required is not token
                    inclusion, but structural realignment: a decisive shift in how we define rigour,
                    interpret method, and assign academic value.


Though this article has taken digital curation as its focal point, the concerns
                    it raises cut across the entire digital scholarly landscape. The exclusions it
                    traces, the devaluation of infrastructure, the suspicion of collaboration, the
                    invisibilisation of process, are not exclusive to curated archives. They
                    underwrite the institutional reception of linguistic corpora, digital tools,
                    memory projects, and public-facing platforms. Curation offers a particularly
                    generative site of analysis because it embodies the epistemic tensions at the
                    heart of digital scholarship: between permanence and iteration, authorship and
                    collectivity, structure and critique.


To treat curation as a method is to make a broader claim: that digital
                    scholarship requires a reimagining of what counts as knowledge, and how that
                    knowledge is recognised. It is not the form that must approximate older genres,
                    but the frameworks of evaluation that must evolve. A hybrid evaluative model,
                    grounded in iteration, multilingualism, and infrastructural care, does not
                    dilute standards; it sharpens them. It names the work that has long sustained
                    research in silence. It affirms that metadata is not mere administration, that
                    interface is not mere design, that access is not ancillary to interpretation.
                    This is not a call for parity between digital and print, it is a call to unlearn
                    the hierarchies that have governed scholarly value for too long. The question is
                    not whether digital work meets academic standards, but whether those standards
                    are capacious enough to meet the digital.


At stake is nothing less than the future of the humanities. A field that cannot
                    acknowledge the infrastructural, distributed, and multimodal conditions of its
                    own present risks rendering itself irrelevant to the very publics it claims to
                    serve. To take digital collections seriously is an act of accountability: to
                    memory, to community, to the evolving forms through which knowledge is built and
                    shared. The task before us is to build the frameworks that can meet these newer
                    forms with clarity and resolve.
