# From the Rock Wall to Wikipedia: Sites of
Digital Connection and Repair in the Community-Engaged Classroom

As we sat reviewing Wikipedia pages in the St. Joseph CME Church’s basement next
to the Marian Cheek Jackson Center, Mr. William Gattis, a lifelong resident of
the Tin Top neighborhood in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, gave us some tough but
fair feedback. He explained, this page is beautifully
written, but it glosses over the violence that we lived through. It is too
simple and plain.

Mr. Gattis was referencing our recently created Wikipedia page for the
Pottersfield neighborhood in Chapel Hill, NC. The goal of the page was to
describe the neighborhood that sits just north of UNC-Chapel Hill's campus and
constitutes part of the university’s history that many students and contemporary
residents in Chapel Hill know little about. The version of the page Mr. Gattis
commented on described the neighborhood’s history, beginning with its origins in
the nineteenth century as a labor settlement for enslaved persons working for
the University of North Carolina. It detailed the evolution of the neighborhood
throughout the Civil Rights Era as many of its residents played pivotal roles in
local and, in some instances, even national movements. The page included details
about recent efforts to preserve the community’s history with memorial plaques,
guided tours, and oral histories. It concluded with a description of how the
current community members were diligently working to preserve this history.

This meeting, held March 2025, consisted of faculty, undergraduate, and graduate
students from UNC-Chapel Hill, Jackson Center staff, and key members of the
Jackson Center's Community Review Board of which Mr. Gattis was a central
member. For the most part, the community members in attendance agreed that the
information on the Wikipedia page was factually correct. The people, places, and
dates were accurate. Where the page fell short was in contextualizing the
motivations and larger implications behind significant moments in the
neighborhood’s history — it did not attend to the difficult, inequitable, and
often violent structures of power that wrapped around each date on the neatly
articulated timeline. As Patricia Jackson (Community Review Board member and St.
Joseph CME’s secretary) stated, This Pottersfield [on the
Wikipedia page] is glamorous. The Pottersfield that we knew was thrown away
property that nobody else wanted, that nothing else could happen on and they
said, Ok, you could use this area.

To disrupt these structural issues of inequity in Wikipedia, we developed a
multi-step workflow that centers community knowledge and builds digital
literacies for students and community members. The process consists of six
steps:

1. Site selection by Jackson Center staff and community members
2. Research and drafting by undergraduate students from a course on the
rhetoric of data (ENGL 114) in consultation with DLC staff to meet
Wikipedia’s secondary-source guidelines
3. Collaboration with Jackson Center staff to publish oral history excerpts
on their website — a workaround we invented since Wikipedia prohibits oral
history as a primary source but allows quotes from oral histories on web
pages
4. Revision and refinement of content
5. Publication of pages by ENGL 114 students along with an accompanying
portfolio to meet course requirements
6. Ongoing page revision and long-term oversight by the DLC in collaboration
with community members.

This paper uses our collaborative workflow as a case study to show how community
expertise and university resources can work together to intervene in Wikipedia’s
increasingly powerful knowledge structures. In so doing, we demonstrate how
Wikipedia can serve as an important site for public digital humanities projects.
We begin by outlining our partnership, then discuss issues in Wikipedia and
Wikidata, and end with a more detailed description of our methods of
intervention.

To take advantage of the affordances of Wikipedia and address issues of absence

### Assignment Structure

First taught in Spring 2022, English 114: The Rhetoric of Data fulfilled
requirements in both the English department’s Writing, Editing, and Digital
Publishing concentration and UNC’s Data Science Minor. The course examined
rhetoric, digital media, information, and data while requiring students to
evaluate how data shapes power structures and impacts lived realities. In
creating the Wikipedia project, we aimed to fulfill these course goals, while
helping students learn about the important sites in Chapel Hill history by
supporting the work of the Jackson Center. The assignment consisted of putting
students into groups with defined roles in which they created Wikipedia and
Wikidata pages on a historical site of the Jackson Center’s choosing, and then
individually created a portfolio that showcased their work on the project, a
narrative reflection, and an example of running Wikidata queries related to
linked topics associated with their Wikipedia pages. Students began the semester
exploring key concepts in theories at the intersection of rhetorical studies and
data studies and then moved into the Wikipedia project, which consisted of a
six-week instructional plan that emphasized the community-based nature of the
project by drawing on the expertise of our different team members (see appendix
A).

This plan was designed to model what Schwan and Thomson refer to as a circular flow of knowledge between academia, other
organizations and communities-thomson_2023[^: As Trey Conatser reminds us,

### The Digital Rhetoric of Wikipedia and Barriers to Participation

students were first introduced to the rhetorical affordances and limitations of
hypertext, Search Engine Optimization (SEO), and (quite directly) the processes

#### Information Infrastructure and Wikidata

Together with creating the Wikipedia pages, students also created
accompanying Wikidata pages for each site. They were also responsible for
using Wikidata’s query tool to investigate a dimension of data related to
their chosen site for their individual portfolio that accompanied their
group’s published pages. The last part of our lesson plan focused on
understanding the power and limitations of the structures on Wikidata and
its query tool. Learning about how Wikidata works helps students gain
important data literacy skills such as data processing, digital provenance,
data ethics, and data completeness

Contributing to Wikidata does not involve editorial review,[^: Other users
can, however, change the information tha]

For example, the group of students who created the Wikipedia page for Watts
Restaurant noted that the restaurant marked the site of an important sit-in
protest in 1963 during the Civil Rights Movement. After creating the
Wikipedia page dedicated to explaining this history, the students wanted to
create a property in Wikidata for involvement in the civil rights movement
such that they could run a query that would show them all the important
sites in the Civil Rights Movement in North Carolina. However, no such
property existed, so they would not be able to answer this query. Unlike
other problems in Wikipedia, this was not an issue of an absence of data,
but instead an absence of the data structure to account for such a crucially
important part of American histories. As one student explained in their
final paper, These gaps were not necessarily the result
of the data not existing but were rather the result of poor linking
connections. In turn, this causes an imbalance in power dynamics that
disadvantages marginalized groups of people.

By making use of an instructional design that introduced community experts
into the classroom, the community-based instructional design of the course
mirrored the community-based creation of the Wikipedia and Wikidata pages.

attention to the fraught and complicated ways in which power — often
exercised through definitions of legitimate data — is distributed between
the pedagogical space of the classroom, the institutional contexts of the

Though the course successfully met learning outcomes in digital and data
literacies and created valuable Wikipedia and Wikidata pages about Chapel
Hill’s Black history, our workflow prioritized sustainability beyond
individual semesters. We aimed to avoid the single course project model,
where community partners invest resources in a class only for the
collaboration to end when students leave, treating their work as a
conclusion rather than a foundation for ongoing engagement. Such single
course projects, even those with the best of intentions, contribute to
extractive and exploitative relationships between universities and
communities

This workflow that extended beyond the course was made possible by
positioning the project within an already existing institution (the DLC
Lab). Rivard, who founded the lab in 2018, created a public humanities
initiative within the lab to fund the graduate students working on the
project as well as leverage the resources, expertise, and infrastructure of
the DLC. Having an infrastructural home-base for this project not only
helped secure the funding necessary to retain project staff but also
guaranteed continuity for this project and for this collaboration long term,
demonstrating the lab’s commitment to the Jackson Center that extends far
beyond the limits of a single semester of work.

While the pages created by ENGL 114 students represented good initial
Wikipedia pages that passed Wikipedia editors test of notability, more
editing was necessary to address issues raised by other Wiki editors and to
honor the complexities of the histories the pages discussed. DLC staff
edited each page with attention to the use of sources, paying particular
attention to their historical credibility. Much of the necessary editing
surrounded the use of sources, which partially was a result of students’
lack of training in historical research methods and Wikipedia’s rules of
verifiability. As we reviewed their final pages, we noted that the students
excelled at finding sources that helped them to understand the narrative
history of these sites. A common challenge, however, was their ability to
discern what was a credible secondary source. One page, for instance,
included a citation that was merely a hyperlink to a Word document with a
narrative about the history of the Hargraves Community Center, summarizing
an interview done with a former director of the Center in the 1980s from a
UNC archive

Our class discussions modeled how these documents could lead to stronger
secondary sources, but students needed more practice to understand which
sources met Wikipedia’s requirements. We adjusted our lesson plans for later
sections accordingly. Moreover, because this was a first-year course, many
of our students had not yet had in-depth experience or training with
identifying the kind of credible historical sources necessary for this
particular platform. It was essential to have advanced graduate students
trained in archival methods to extend these undergraduate students’ work.
Wiki editors are vigilant about attending to the principles of
verifiability, thus, the work done by these graduate students allowed us to
ensure that all sources were vetted.

After DLC staff further edited the pages, they presented the pages to the
Community Review Board (CRB) at the Jackson Center, and then held open
community meetings where anyone community members affiliated with the
Jackson Center could attend. Links to all of the pages as well as printed
copies were shared beforehand so that participants could review the work
that had been done. In these meetings, community members were asked to give
feedback on the pages and were also informed of the Wikipedia rules of
editing and page creation. Many CRB members had already been watching and
discussing the pages’ progress as updates were routinely shared by Wall
throughout the process. CRB member Pat Jackson relayed the community’s
excitement, commenting that one of the pages was talked
about at [a local restaurant] for two weeks.

### Conclusion

Ensuring that the histories we collaboratively created remain on Wikipedia
requires extensive research (and related time and resources) as it is often
difficult to find sources that support community members’ knowledge as much of
this history has not been cataloged and preserved because of structural
inequities in library and archive systems. Another instance at this same meeting
on the Pottersfield neighborhood Wikipedia page evidences a research difficulty
that we found too common. There was a strong consensus among all members present
that Black residents were originally relegated to Pottersfield because it was a
geographically undesirable area in the late nineteenth century mostly due to
routine flooding that occurred, often experiencing significant run-off from the
adjacent university. Topographical maps make it clear that Pottersfield was
established in a place that was undesirable agriculturally and sat at a lower
elevation -of-chapel-hill-chapel-hill-weekly

In such cases, Wikipedia’s editorial guidelines make it nearly impossible to
recount history accurately because they require editors to ground claims in
secondary sources that not only exclude the lived experience of marginalized
communities but have historically served to actively further the interests of
dominant groups. The result is a recollection of historical events that is often
unable to adequately represent the experience of communities like the one in
Chapel Hill. Despite the efforts of Black Chapel Hill residents to honor and
preserve their own history through extensive oral history projects, walking
tours, and community events and resources, many of the records they have created
do not meet Wikipedia’s requirements for credible-sources

As Wikipedia continues to shape AI training data and search results, the stakes
of these editorial norms only grow higher. Our ongoing collaboration suggests
that sustained, reciprocal partnerships between universities and communities can
help intervene in these knowledge structures, but such interventions require
institutional support, dedicated resources, and a willingness to push against
Wikipedia’s boundaries. Most importantly, they require centering community
voices not just as sources to be cited, but as experts whose knowledge deserves
recognition within digital knowledge infrastructures. It is our hope that this
paper provides some practical tips about how to forge collaborative projects
that pool resources as well as how to push against the powerful boundaries of
Wikipedia to create more robust, accurate, and inclusive knowledge
structures.

### Appendix A: Wikipedia/Data Project Overview

present your Wikipedia and Wikidata pages together during our final exam period.
In addition to the group work, each person will individually turn in a portfolio

3. Include at least 4 sources from each person in the group (for example,

include external links.
4. Include at least one image
5. Include an Infobox
6. Include the following sections: Lead section, body section entitled

Note: if a different title/subtitle organization is necessary
for your Wikipedia page, you can get approval for the
alternative structure
7. Be at least 500 words long, not including the references

#### Requirements for Wikidata pages

Each Wikidata page must:

1. Include at least 2 Wikidata properties from each person in the group

implementation plans of a collaborative project. It generally defines the
overall program goals and describes the collaborative nature and relationship
between the identified project and MOU-referenced participant.

disputes by clarifying the expectations of the partners. The process of

partners.
