### The stack


What is a stack? It is first of all a technical model (for instance the OSI
                    model), which sets out how systems like the internet operate. It consists of
                    hierarchical layers, with each layer abstracting upwards, handling functions
                    independently, and defining principles for interaction. Potentially fostering
                    platform agnosticism (or interoperability) and promoting ideas of layered
                    emergence, it can also promote compartmentalized thinking. The stack has
                    variously been taken up as a way of modelling extant systems, as a technical
                    diagram, as a global cybernetic order, as a metaphor, as a means through which
                    to articulate a demand, as a manifesto, and as one of the ways the relationship
                    between digital transformation and social justice has been explored in recent
                    times (see e.g. ([[Bratton 2016]]) ([[Flanders 2018]]) ([[Liu 2020]]) ([[Terranova 2014]])
                    ([[Berry 2017]]) ([[Bassett et al. 2019]])). Stack approaches to exploring technological
                    systems are thus contested and operate in different registers.


In this paper, we use a stack model to point to the material affordances of
                    extant digital architectures – specifically including those found in archives
                    and doing so with an understanding of the degree to which technological
                    imaginaries operate powerfully at multiple levels and in many materialities. We
                    also do so with cognisance of the ways the stack has also been adopted as a
                    powerful metaphor, both analytic and political (and at times how this has been
                    done by productively confounding the division between the metaphorical and
                    material). We return to this below. The move now is to ask what is lacking in
                    intersectional feminisms’*current entanglements with DH.*

### Back to the stack


Stacks have already been introduced, but here demand some elaboration. Amongst
                    other things, stacks have been employed as a way of understanding how the
                    internet works as a layered system. The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection)
                    reference model, which deals with the internet and associated networks, and what
                    enables interconnection between them, is one example of this, and has become
                    somewhat canonical. It consists of layers rising from the physical base towards
                    the lighter super-structures, an ordering often understood in terms of
                    etherealization (from the physical depths, up to the application layers enabling
                    human computer interaction). What are also modelled are the principles
                    controlling the intersections between various layers. The system abstracts
                    upwards and is modular, each layer handing off functions to the layer above. In
                    theory at least, the principles of heritance and autonomy between layers means
                    that what happens on one layer is not the business of the next.


We are interested in how intersectional feminist digital politics may be
                    configured within extant material conditions and possibilities, those that the
                    stack model and its layers might help us understand. The stack models the real
                    and stack politics reflect that. We are also interested in the ideological heft
                    of the model; in particular, in the way it might, in so far as it has become a
                    naturalized way of understanding the affordances of the different technical
                    layers of network systems, imply that there is a proper*as* political rather than technical). Finally, we believe
                    there is potential for the stack to be used metaphorically within a feminist
                    digital politics. In all these ways, we believe stacks can be useful for DH as
                    it seeks to think through the specifically technical elements of a form of
                    (feminist) (intersectional) (historically materialist) politics, whilst also
                    understanding those technical elements as techno-cultural.


Specifically, we wish to explore the prospects for a depth model of
                    intersectional feminism; one that foregrounds the material possibilities and
                    constraints of operations at particular and multiple levels of the stack and
                    recognizes technical heritance, but that also (i) seeks to refuse conventional
                    assignations that arise when stack layers are mistaken for social structures and
                    conventions (and vice versa), and (ii) that stresses the connective tissues that
                    link operations undertaken at different levels in stacked systems *in operation* or *in location*, which

### Community archives


Large archives and institutional repositories are facing radical digital
                    disruption, and also changing in response to cultural shifts as well as demands
                    for archival justice coming from multiple directions, including those engaging
                    with intersectional feminisms. Archives and archival practices are in many other

To think about an intersectional feminist stack politics, as this relates to
                    archival activities and more generally, is thus to refuse the temptation to
                    enfold feminist activities around archival issues into sets of demands that can
                    be simply and comfortably resolved, but that do not address the complexity of
                    the technical inheritances of the systems we work within, nor the ways in which
                    they are also non-neutral. It is also to insist on defining the object in
                    relation to the circulation that its digital form allows. At the interface
                    layers, the social structures that condition meaning are more clearly
                    discernible as legacies carried into code than those further down the stack.
                    These might appear to be evident only on that layer. Nevertheless, they are
                    connected through the stack, via the technical process(es) of handing off,
                    whereby infrastructure conditions layers above it. And, they are connected as
                    well as in more indirect and lateral ways, through network architectures on a
                    global scale having been forged, in their incompleteness, their unevenness, in
                    their gateways and closures, in relation to cultural and social histories and
                    conflicts, and struggles around justice around gender, race, class and sexuality
                    and its structural withholding.


An intersectional feminist approach to archiving, based on the demand for care,
                    allows for both critique, intervention and mobilisation, but all of these
                    require a holistic view of the materials and processes entailed in making and
                    using archives, their practical and symbolic affordances. It must understand the
                    impact of the inheritances up and down the stack in order to fully appreciate
                    the complexity of any holding or object – understood as an archival object. It
                    must also understand these dynamics as producing a particular, situated material
                    reality but one that can be analysed as discrete moments; it retains the
                    specificity of discriminations manifesting in a particular layer while insisting
                    that these layers are imbricated together. Finally, it must understand the power
                    and force of arguments that claim large parts of the social reality that is
                    computational culture have become inevitable, unchangeable, and are not amenable
                    to feminist demands.


### Conclusion


Thinking about intersectional feminism and DH through stacks isn’t a panacea.
                    There are issues with the formalism of the model; the temptation is real to
                    simply take the stack (model) for the reality of complex and layered
                    techno-social systems that make up the digital archive archipelago emerging as
                    digitalisation processes intensify; and this is not a matter of mixing metaphor
                    with material, but rather assuming that the model of a system becomes its
                    reality. However, we find it helpful to use it to think through or hold together
                    a set of considerations of the necessarily intersectional modes of feminism at
                    play in contemporary global society with all its multiple forms of
                    discrimination.


Our exploration of the stack concept has led us to explore a series of questions
                    about *where* DH feminism should operate – at which levels,
                    and in what ways. Informed by the IFTe Network, we have expanded upon our stack
                    orientation in our subsequent project, **Full Stack Feminism
                        in Digital Humanities**[^: Full Stack Feminism in Digital Humanities
                        (2021-24) was ]answer


By employing the stack analogy, whether through the FSF framework or earlier
                    iterations, we provoke critical reflection on where, within the stack or layer,
                    a sense of intersectional politics in DH truly belongs. In this context,
                    questions about the place of intersectional feminisms arise, and various
                    perspectives have offered diverse suggestions on how to address this issue.
                    Exploring these propositions, in the context of archives and on issues arising
                    around archival practice, we wanted to both illuminate the material conditions
                    we found and also use the example to challenge some assumptions about stack
                    hierarchy. Feminisms need to operate to grapple with the questions DH raises
                    both for itself and as – or in so far as – it wishes to engage with digital
                    cultures in a critical and questioning way that seeks justice. *All that seems solid may melt…*
