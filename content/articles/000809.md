# Gapping the Map: Indigenous Presence in Native Land
Digital

### Introduction

Native Land Digital (NLD) is a Canadian Indigenous-led non-profit organization. Their
digital interactive project continuously reminds us of the question that lies at its
heart: *Whose land are we on?* By mapping Indigenous
territories, peoples, languages, and treaties, NLD actively challenges dominant
colonial narratives of land ownership that have historically subjugated conventional
understandings of geographic history. By critically analyzing NLD’s approach,
methodology, and audience, this review demonstrates how the project redefines digital
mapping as a medium for Indigenous self-representation, while also highlighting the
strengths and shortcomings that arise within both public and scholarly conversation
on digital mapping projects.

The NLD project situates itself at the intersection of Indigenous studies,
postcolonial theory and spatial humanities. It thereby operates in a growing global
movement of mapping Indigenous lands digitally. NLD exists alongside a plethora of
similar projects that emerged in the context of digital mapping such as the
Indigenous Mapping Collective, First Nations Location, and Native Land Information
System, just to name a few. The non-profit collective **Rest of

### Versatility

As stated in the introduction, NLD falls under the category of Indigenous studies,
postcolonial theory and spatial humanities. Its interactive map serves as an
extension of quantitative history and derives its methodology from history and
archaeology in the humanities, visualized by digital tools such as mapping,
hypermedia links, and data visualization. Additionally, printable lists of
territories, languages, and treaties serve as a textual database. The project is
fairly intuitive to navigate. A search bar enables users to explore specific regions
and the selecting as well as un-selecting of languages, territories and/or treaties
allows for a more tailored investigation of these categories. Once selected,
hyperlinks navigate the user to external pages about the nations and histories of an
area. Additionally, the project offers the possibility to export the page’s data to
printable image files. The landing page of NLD, however, can at times appear dense
and visually complex. The color coding of different Indigenous nations overlaps on
the map, which creates a blur of names, colors, and borders. From an analytical point
of view, one could argue that this feature highlights the multifaceted and rich
history of countries we deem as monocultural. In terms of accessibility, however, the
pastel-colors can be difficult to navigate and differentiate, especially for
colorblind users. It is possible to turn off the color section altogether, which,
however, defies the purpose of the overlappings of the map. As NLD has been an
ongoing project for a decade now, these visual tools have expanded over time. NLD was
founded by Victor Temprano in 2014 -land_2024a

Since it was founded in 2014, NLD has not only grown in size, but also in community.
Temprano stepped down as the executive director in 2022 and was replaced by Māori
designer Tanya Ruka. NLD now additionally consists of an Indigenous board of
directors and an advisory council of both Indigenous and non-Indigenous members -land_2024b

The vast data sets compiled by staff, volunteers, and research projects at several
universities in Canada are analyzed and visualized by several digital tools. Until
2022, NLD pushed to and pulled from the Mapbox dataset into a custom-made WordPress
plugin

### Absences

Given the theoretical framework of the project, it is essential for this review to
explore the interactive map in action. The map is organized in a globe-structure that
can be moved to and from every angle, deconstructing the euro-centric view on
traditional world maps. Before accessing the map, the audience is presented with a
Native Land and Territorial Acknowledgement and the goal of the project to [honor] Indigenous resilience past, present and future-land_2024amap

### Community

The collaborations of NLD are manifold. Besides the numerous volunteers, who are
encouraged to submit evidence of Indigenous territories, languages, or treaties, NLD
partners with organizations such as Digital Democracy and Historica Canada. Notably,
the project closely collaborates with other digital humanities projects such as Whose
Land and Tribal Nations Maps -land_2024-land_2024b-land_2024c-land_2024a

The NLD project engages a variety of users; Indigenous newscasts such as CBC News
praise NLD for their work and careful research, and **HuffPost****The Guardian**-land_2024a

Given the NLD’s reliance on both institutional funding and volunteer contributions,
concerns emerge not only about the source, but also about the storage of its data.
Although the project has moved to one SQL database and away from scattered data sets
in 2022, the aggregation of data from such a plethora of sources carries significant
risks of inaccuracy. A strength of the map, however, is the fact that it is possible
to look up when which alterations were made to the map. Its continuous changes assert
that NLD continues to evolve through global volunteer work and activism despite an
apparent outward halt.

The ability to track such evolving changes on the map provides insight into global
cultural, historical, and political processes. Its evolving representation invites
humanities scholars to examine how we can use digital tools to assess narratives of
land ownership and identity formation. For American Studies scholars, the NLD project
offers a valuable perspective on the intersection of mapping, representation, and
sovereignty. As Dunst examines in his essay on digital American Studies, our
disciplinary field can only benefit from what digital humanities projects have to
offer, mainly establishing access and providing orientation,
organizing communities online, and enlarging discussions in digital and analog
spaces
