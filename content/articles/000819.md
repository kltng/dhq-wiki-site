### Definitions and scope

The term web map component.d..d..d.

Three visual features are considered core to the component namely tiles, vectors and
projection. Tiles form the base or background images of the web map whilst vectors
take the form of points, polylines, or polygons, commonly styled as markers or shapes
that sit on top of tiles. Vectors’ underlying data consist of geometries, usually in
the form of coordinates, and attributes, text, URLs, or other information attached to
the coordinates. The final visual feature is projection, a cartographic method used
to transform the curved surface of the earth so that it can be viewed on a flat,
two-dimensional one such as a screen. Interactivity is possible through a
circumscribed set of actions that facilitate two goals: update of the visual display
and the retrieval and display of attribute data. The former is achieved through
zooming and panning actions that adjust the position and focus of vectors and tiles
on screen. Attribute data can be retrieved by clicking on a vector. Particular
implementations of web map components may, of course, contain other features such as
a title, north arrow, text search, or scale; however, these are not essential.
Similarly, these components might be embedded within a broader web page that
facilitates further interactions through filters, sliders or buttons.

### Interactive features

Decisions taken in designing these features can provide signifiers that emphasise
particular affordances. Such signifiers are particularly important as researchers do
not necessarily have a use-case in mind. Digital humanities scholarship has argued
that assuming users have a predetermined goal is unrealistic and counter-productive
without any clear purpose, rambling through the data in the hope of
encountering something interestinginsight

The widespread use of web maps leads users to possess pre-existing knowledge of
affordances. It is unnecessary to provide signifiers that panning, moving the
geographical display of the map, is possible by clicking, holding and moving the
mouse laterally or longitudinally. Similarly, users will know that turning the mouse
wheel facilitates zooming in and out. However, the developer should always consider
constraining the geographical area that can be panned whilst limiting the ability to
zoom. The affordance that vectors offer to retrieve attribute data through a click is
familiar to users from previous experience. Most would be unsurprised when their
click triggers the appearance of a popup in the browser displaying text, a hyperlink,
an image or a video. The motivation for retrieving attribute data differs based on
use case. In visual analysis, attributes point to evidence on
which the argument is based

The developer has several opportunities to provide signifiers that guide the user.
For example, vectors’ representations can be tailored to emphasise that they are
clickable by providing a symbol of sufficient size and opacity or by employing tool
tips. More complex vector designs such as heatmaps (see Figure 2) or
choropleths[^: A thematic map that displays statistical information as
colour].d.

Signifying the importance of these hyperlinks is the most important consideration
when designing for discovery. Furthermore, previews that communicate properties of

### Visual features

Of all the features of the web map component, the developer has the greatest control
over vectors, coordinates displayed as markers and other shapes on the map. The
vector data consumed by the web map component takes the point, polyline, and polygon
forms that are known from GIS. The underlying geometry or coordinates are commonly
encoded in WGS84: the most commonly used system for representing locations on the
earth’s surface as latitude and longitude. These coordinates then sit within standard
data structures designed to share geospatial data on the web that include formats
such as GeoJSON, KML or the OGC’s Web Feature Service (WFS). For example, Peripleo is
based on the Linked Places standard developed in association with the Pelagios
Network. Some DH applications make the underlying datasets available for download,
Pauleicia offers this ability providing opportunities for reuse-2.0_n.d..d.

The representation of vectors should vary according to use case in terms of form and
style. Web maps offer developers opportunities to configure symbol, shape, colour, or
shade to represent attributes and support visual analysis. Users have experience of
using particular styles for certain forms of visual analysis, for instance,
choropleths in the case of electoral data. The division of a dataset into layers
means vectors can be broken down into subsets where differences in representation
might communicate relationships

Whilst vectors should be the focus of any web map, tiles dominate the display space,
setting the visual tone. Tiles provide an efficient and lightweight method of
displaying and updating background maps in the browser based on zoom and pan
interactions.[^: Tiles are requested individually based on zoom and pan

Temporality is a critical consideration in the use of tiles in the digital humanities
yet has been little discussed. Designed for use in today’s society, tiles generally
depict current geography. Yet humanities research examines varied time periods making
the use of many tiles anachronistic. Despite these concerns, such tiles are widely

choice of projection a critical consideration in designing any web map. A projection

possible to use many different calculations to display or approximate the sphere that
is the earth on a flat surface. That map projections should be

being mapped, and the types of measurements that a reader might need to

### Concluding remarks

The user-centred approach adopted by this article has led to a set of recommendations
for designing web maps to support the two use cases. Designing for the discovery use
case supports finding and accessing research objects and draws on researchers’
experience of using web maps elsewhere. Discovery aligns with the priorities of the
creators of digital research objects including research projects and cultural
heritage organisations who are interested in maximising the use of web pages through
usage metrics and usability studies. Collections of research objects and the
resulting datasets are palimpsests of decisions; their partial, subjective nature
might not be suitable for collective interpretation. Designing for discovery can
guide the user away from visual analysis and help projects and organisations to
present a breadth of research objects. This article suggests that the implementation
of web maps for visual analysis requires careful thought. The design of visual
features such as vectors, tiles, and projection should be considered in relation to
the dataset and aims of the project. These aims do appear to be unclear for many
projects for which simply making a map available appears to be the main goal. The
article’s perspective on visual analysis has been cultivated in response to the
author’s work on MJCBE. Here, the web map component and associated interactive
functionality were designed to support visual analysis. Yet use of the interface for
this purpose has not proven straightforward and relies on significant background
knowledge of the Byzantine empire. There have been few citations that record usage of
the MJCBE interface for its intended purpose in the decade since release which raises
the question of how to measure the success or effectiveness of web maps in the visual
analysis use case. This article does not argue that every web map could be designed
exclusively for visual analysis or discovery. Maps might serve both purposes or
others such as the visualisation of a single narrative, journey, or marker or
offering access to statistical information such as calculating routes in the past

Future work, encompassing a systematic investigation of more projects that have used
web map components could categorise implementations in terms of their data type,
theme or subject, vector representation, and other characteristics leading to further
definitions of use cases. The article has adopted a mechanistic or instrumentalist
perspective focused on activities, tasks, and features – a perspective which might be
considered to be at odds with recent digital humanities literature that persuasively
argues for a reconsideration of the task-based or goal-oriented paradigm that has
pervaded user experience or visualisation research of knowing through interpretative work
