This article describes CCC’s capacity for exposing these under-examined patterns

the CCC dataset: many states’ prisons restrict romance/erotica and manga
publications by labeling them sexually explicit. In what follows, we first

### 1. CCC as Critical Making

The CCC database evolved as part of Ka’s artistic reckoning with structural
barriers that drastically limit incarcerated people’s access to reading
material. Her ongoing examination of this topic shapeshifts in response to each
iteration’s analytical focal points, participants, and spaces (physical,
virtual, and/or discursive). CCC stems from Ka’s open-ended critical making
approach to prison censorship and carceral exchange. In particular, CCC’s
information architecture showcases her creative and critical methods for
adopting digital formats. It breaks with default web templates to aesthetically
convey her database’s conceptual and contextual specificities. As we will
detail, CCC presents data in evocative yet orderly geometric compositions,
including a matrix presentation that nods to the history of computing via the
punch card matrix.

#### 1.1. CCC’s Information Architecture

CCC aims to raise awareness about US carceral information exchange and
censorship. This project leans into a data-driven format more than Ka’s
previous ones on the same topic, while still maintaining the expressive
concerns of her social art practice. She conceived CCC not only as a
methodical user-friendly representation of copious data about US prisons’
banned publications, but also as a critical entry point into this topic.

CCC’s visuals refer to governmental and computational systems of
meaning-making. The database uses arrangements of rectangles where each
rectangle serves as a data point and signifies a single banned prison
publication. Together, the rectangles map US prison bans according to
different geographic scopes. For example, Ka’s national display pictures
individual publications together through a matrix of rectangles.

CCC’s entirely grayscale color palette suggests the governmental paperwork
behind its own data collection. Moreover, the database’s matrix composition
draws inspiration from the rectangular matrix of punch cards used for
proto-computing data processing. Ka thought of Herman Hollerith’s punch
card, devised for the eleventh US census of 1890. One such machine-readable
punch card represented one US resident’s data. Each hole punch mark within
the columns and rows on the card stood for a data point relating to that
resident’s characteristics, such as their sex, race, employment, marital
status, etc.

#### 1.2. Navigating Social Identity Categories with CCC

Ka’s integration of visual traces from this punch-card-to-IBM trajectory
evokes computational methods’ socio-political ramifications. In keeping with
these methods, her datasets also rely on social identity categories of race
and ethnicity as well as gender. She uses these categories to shed light on
racist and sexist censorship practices corresponding to authors’ identities.
Nevertheless, like the social categories of the 1890 census, Ka’s are based
on hegemonic constructs and do not represent the vast range of ways people
self-identify their race, ethnicity, and gender. A lack of preexisting
structured data on publication authors' self-identification in relation to
these attributes is one of the many challenges Ka negotiates when creating
her database. Here, as Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein’s remark
[t]he absence of data,becomes an important
takeaway, as meaningful as the data themselves

CCC directly applies the 2020 US census categories of race and ethnicity to
its author data but takes more liberties with this census’s binary “sex”
categories of male and female. Ka revises them as “gender” categories of men
(which includes transmen), women (which includes transwomen), uncertain (for
authors whose gender the CCC team cannot determine), non-binary, and
publisher (when authorship is not attributed to people but instead the
publisher). Whereas sex refers to how people identify biologically, Ka’s
shift to gender departs from reductive biologically determined definitions
of identity in favor of more inclusive and complex understandings of it.
Gender refers to culturally constructed notions of femininity and
masculinity that reflect an interplay between a person’s self-presentation
and others’ perception of it

Ka also developed a website mechanism for illustrating how race, ethnicity,
and gender intersect with censorship practices. To explore banned
publication authors by social identity, users can select categories of
gender as well as race and ethnicity. These categories each overlay texture
visualizations onto the existing data. Additionally, the database includes
categories that present DOC designated reasons for each publication’s
censorship with another kind of texture.

### 2. Censoring romance/Erotica and Manga’s Sexual Speech in and beyond US
Prisons

According to our research, these under-examined censorship patterns indicate that
standards differentiating between acceptable and unacceptable sexual content are
rarely as neutral as they might first appear. Even when the guidelines for
banning sexually explicit or obscene content seem unbiased, US authorities’
application of them often devalues socially marginalized people and
perspectives. The heightened prison censorship of women’s and East Asians’
sexual speech via romance/erotica literature and manga we see in Ka’s initial
findings offer two telling examples. As previously noted, CCC data derive from a
synthesis of sources: DOC materials (such as their banned book lists, their
censorship codes, and publication censorship reports) that provide censorship
reasons, the CCC team’s research on authors’ identities, and the American
Library Association designations for different kinds of publications (such as
manga as a type of publication and romance and erotica as genres). CCC’s
database provides all the data for the categories and analyses discussed as well
as visualized in this article, but CCC’s website does not currently represent
all these categories and analyses.

These prison censorship patterns are novel CCC-powered findings, but biased US
censorship practices in the US are nothing new. Hegemonic identity-group
concerns steered early US censorship policies of sexually explicit content. The
US Constitution’s First Amendment protects free speech and prohibits the
government from favoring any religions. Nevertheless, throughout the nation’s
history, many courts have suppressed sexually suggestive material to uphold
Christian morality. During the turn of the nineteenth century, Evangelicals of
the Second Great Awakening introduced bans of such material beyond the pale,
calling it obscenity. They attempted to use the state’s power to curb what they
perceived as sin[king] it a crime to knowingly mail obscene
materials or advertisements and information about obscene materials,
abortion, or contraception[^: After the Supreme Court first directly ruled on
obscene ]

The US Supreme Court’s current guidelines for censoring obscene material
constitute one of these secular and seemingly neutral mandates. Established in
1973, these guidelines are known as the Miller test. Through the test, states
can designate content obscene and therefore unprotected by the First Amendment.
To be judged obscene, content must fit not just one or two, but all three of the
following characterizations:

1. the average person, applying contemporary community
standards, would find that the work, taken as a whole, appeals to the
prurient interest
2. the work depicts or describes, in a patently offensive
way, sexual conduct or excretory functions specifically defined by
applicable state law
3. the work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary,
artistic, political, or scientific value-vs-california_1973

Again, the First Amendment protects sexual speech, i.e., any form of
communication about sexual matters, which passes the Miller test.

Yet in and beyond prison, censors frequently ban sexual speech in romance/erotica
and manga even when it passes the Miller test. We propose that such double
standards stem from censors’ discriminatory appraisals of the sexual speech that
these publications engage. To be clear, we do not assume all the enforcers of
prison censorship mean to push an ideologically suppressive or anti-woke agenda.
Some may, but plenty are just responding to the bureaucratic pressures of
maintaining a semblance of order and efficiency in the carceral system

#### 2.2 US Censorship and Manga

In addition to revealing that numerous prison bans on romance and erotica
detract from incarcerated people’s access to women’s sexual speech, Ka’s
preliminary data analysis shows that prisons throughout the nation often
reject manga. In so doing, these prisons reject a great deal of publications
by people of East Asian descent who make up the majority of the banned
manga’s authors. Just as restrictions on romance and erotica outside the
carceral system relate to those inside it, prisons’ penchant for prohibiting
manga reflects this publication type’s commonly censored status within US
society at large.

### Conclusion

Among DH projects about the prison industrial complex, CCC is distinct for its
critical artistic construction led by inquiry into social bias. CCC partakes in
Ka’s socially engaged art practice, experiments with information architecture,
and tracks the US prison censorship of numerous publication types for further
inspection. Its unique web design promotes not only an interactive
user-experience, but also an evocative one, as its visuals reference the
governmental and computational contexts of its own data. Moreover, CCC invites
users to explore perspectives that prison publication bans target and why. As
the website partially now shows, the database and its categories reveal
relationships between prison officials’ censorship justifications, censored
publications and their types, and censored authors’ social identities at the
local institutional, state, and national level within the US. Concentrating on
the sexually explicit justification for censorship, CCC’s initial findings
uncover widespread banning habits that devalue views associated with women’s and
East Asians’ sexual speech (through the prison censorship of romance/erotica
literature and manga). CCC’s ability to make these two overlooked prison
censorship patterns detectable illustrates how Ka’s database, and by extension
DH more broadly, can galvanize efforts to articulate and agitate against
discrimination within the prison industrial complex.

By animating the database’s inquiry into social biases through her artistic
treatment of it, Ka illuminates how DH and socially engaged art practices can be
mutually enriching. The former offers data-driven methods for strengthening the
analytical aspects and reach of the latter beyond the art field. In turn,
socially engaged art’s creative means of reckoning with social justice issues
can invigorate DH strategies.
