# Machine Learning in DHQ

## Overview

Machine learning appears as a keyword in 64 DHQ articles, representing an important methodological strand within the journal's coverage. DHQ articles on machine learning span the period from early work in computational stylistics and NLP to more recent applications of deep learning, classification, clustering, and topic modeling in humanities research. The journal has been a venue for demonstrating both the power and the limitations of ML methods when applied to humanities questions.

A distinctive feature of DHQ's ML coverage is its attention to the *interpretive gap* — the space between what machine learning can detect (statistical patterns) and what humanistic scholarship requires (meaningful interpretations). DHQ authors consistently argue that ML is most useful when it serves as a provocation to interpretation rather than as a replacement for it.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Classification and Stylistics

The dominant paradigm in DHQ's ML coverage is text classification: using machine learning algorithms (primarily SVM, naive Bayes, and decision trees) to classify texts by author gender, nationality, race, genre, or period. The ARTFL group's studies are exemplary: they demonstrate high classification accuracy while acknowledging that "the most successful classifications were accomplished by normalizing the data in various ways" which "creates a kind of distance from the text as originally composed" and may "limit the interpretive utility of classification tools" (DHQ:000043).

### NLP for Historical Languages

David Bamman and Gregory Crane's work on classical lexicography (DHQ:000033) demonstrates how ML can be applied to the particular challenges of historical languages — morphological analysis, lemmatization, and disambiguation in Ancient Greek and Latin. This work shows that ML methods developed for modern languages require significant adaptation for historical contexts.

### Theoretical Reflections on ML Agency

John Johnston's "Webbots and Machinic Agency" (DHQ:000126) moves beyond application to theoretical reflection, examining how ML systems exhibit forms of agency that complicate traditional understandings of authorship, intentionality, and textual production. This article represents a more critical strand in DHQ's ML coverage that questions rather than simply applies computational methods.

### The Humanistic ML Paradigm

Across DHQ's coverage, a distinctive "humanistic ML" paradigm emerges: one that uses ML not to produce definitive classifications but to generate "provocations" for interpretation — patterns that demand explanation, anomalies that reveal assumptions, and features that open new lines of inquiry.
