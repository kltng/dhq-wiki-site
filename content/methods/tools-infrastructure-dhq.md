# Tools and Infrastructure in DHQ

## Overview

The cluster of tools (147 articles), infrastructure (93), and users represents approximately 314 articles — the largest methodological cluster in DHQ. This coverage addresses the technical systems, software platforms, standards, and user-facing tools that underpin digital humanities work. DHQ articles on tools and infrastructure range from detailed technical descriptions of specific software to theoretical reflections on how tool design shapes scholarly practice.

A central argument across DHQ's tools/infrastructure coverage is that tools are not neutral instruments but embody theoretical assumptions about their objects of study. John Bradley's work on annotation tools (DHQ:000121), TEI encoding practices, and database design all reflect the principle that infrastructure choices are intellectual choices.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Cyberinfrastructure as Scholarly Necessity

The classics cluster (DHQ:000023, DHQ:000035) makes the most sustained argument for cyberinfrastructure as essential to humanities research. Gregory Crane and colleagues argue that the comprehensive digitization, encoding, NLP processing, and linked data integration of classical texts requires infrastructure on a scale comparable to scientific cyberinfrastructure — and that this infrastructure, once built, will transform not just classics but the humanities as a whole.

### The "Perpetual Prototype" Problem

William Kretzschmar (DHQ:000038) and others address the sustainability challenge: large-scale DH infrastructure projects often struggle with funding continuity, technological obsolescence, and institutional commitment. The tension between innovation and sustainability is a recurring concern.

### Tools as Theory

John Bradley's "Towards a Richer Sense of Digital Annotation" (DHQ:000121) argues that annotation tools embody particular theories of what annotation *is* — and that current tools, oriented toward a "media" model of digital objects, may not adequately support the kinds of annotation that humanities scholars actually need. This exemplifies a broader DHQ argument: tool design is never neutral.

### User-Centered Design

Several DHQ articles attend to the "users" keyword, examining how scholars actually use (or fail to use) digital tools. This user-centered perspective reveals that the most technically sophisticated tool is worthless if it does not align with scholars' existing practices, conceptual frameworks, and workflow preferences.

### Standards and Interoperability

The TEI (Text Encoding Initiative) looms large in DHQ's infrastructure coverage, as do discussions of XML standards, metadata schemas, linked data protocols, and interoperability frameworks. These standards are presented not as mere technical conveniences but as enabling conditions for scholarly communication and collaboration.
