# Digital History in DHQ

## Overview

History is a keyword in 158 DHQ articles, making it one of the most prominent disciplinary markers in the journal. DHQ's digital history coverage spans historical GIS, digital archives for historical research, computational analysis of historical sources, public history, and theoretical reflections on how digital methods transform historical scholarship. The journal documents a field in which historians are not merely consumers of digital tools but active shapers of digital methods and infrastructures.

DHQ's digital history coverage is distinctive for its attention to *spatial humanities* — the use of GIS, mapping, and geospatial analysis to understand historical processes. From the mapping of Concord's literary geography (DHQ:000057) to the digital geography of the classical world (DHQ:000031), DHQ shows how spatial analysis opens new dimensions of historical understanding.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Spatial History and GIS

Tom Elliott and Sean Gillies (DHQ:000031) demonstrate how digital geography transforms the study of classical history, using GIS to map ancient places, routes, and relationships. Amy Earhart's mapping of Concord (DHQ:000057) applies similar methods to American literary history, showing how geospatial visualization can reveal patterns invisible in traditional textual analysis.

### Historical Data Modeling

Bruce Robertson's Heml project (DHQ:000026) uses RDF and semantic web technologies to model historical events and their relationships, arguing that structured data representation enables new forms of historical inquiry. This exemplifies a broader DHQ theme: the modeling of historical data is itself a form of historical argument.

### Large-Scale Historical Computing

William Kretzschmar (DHQ:000038) addresses the challenges of building and sustaining large-scale historical computing projects, drawing on his experience with the Linguistic Atlas Project. His article highlights the tension between the long time horizons of historical research and the short-term funding cycles that typically support digital projects.

### Public History and Digital Access

Drew VandeCreek (DHQ:000003) exemplifies DHQ's attention to public history — the use of digital archives and tools to make historical knowledge accessible beyond the academy. This connects digital history to broader questions of access, democratization, and the public role of humanities scholarship.

### Oral History and Digital Methods

DHQ also covers the intersection of oral history and digital methods, including Julianne Nyhan and Andrew Flinn's "Hidden Histories" project (DHQ:000130), which uses digital methods to record and preserve the oral history of digital humanities itself — a reflexive gesture that underscores the journal's commitment to historical self-awareness.
