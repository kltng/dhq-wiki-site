# Corpus Methods in DHQ

## Overview

Corpora is a keyword in 64 DHQ articles, representing the journal's sustained engagement with corpus-based methods for humanities research. DHQ's coverage spans corpus creation, annotation, analysis, and interpretation, addressing both the practical challenges of building and maintaining text corpora and the theoretical implications of treating literature, history, and culture as collections of computationally processable texts.

Corpus methods in DHQ are closely linked to computational stylistics, authorship attribution, text mining, and distant reading. The journal has been a primary venue for the ARTFL Project's influential work on French literary corpora, as well as for work on classical, medieval, and modern literary corpora.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Corpus Construction as Scholarly Activity

DHQ articles consistently argue that building a scholarly corpus is itself an interpretive act. Decisions about what to include, how to represent texts, and what metadata to record shape the kinds of questions that can be asked and the kinds of answers that can be found. The Suda On Line project (DHQ:000025) exemplifies how corpus creation can be a distributed scholarly activity, engaging dozens of collaborators in translating and annotating a massive Byzantine reference work.

### From Small Corpora to Big Data

DHQ documents a shift from small, carefully curated corpora (individual author studies, specific literary periods) to large-scale collections that enable "distant reading" approaches. The ARTFL Project's work on French literature and the Black Drama database represent a scale of corpus analysis that would be impossible without computational methods.

### Corpus Annotation and Representation

How texts are represented in a corpus — what level of annotation is applied, what features are marked up, what metadata is recorded — profoundly affects what can be discovered. DHQ articles on TEI encoding, part-of-speech tagging, and morphological analysis highlight the centrality of corpus annotation to humanities computing.

### Stylistics and Authorship Attribution

David Hoover's work (DHQ:000012) exemplifies the use of corpus methods for statistical stylistics and authorship attribution — using computational analysis of linguistic features to identify authorial signatures, genre conventions, and stylistic change over time.
