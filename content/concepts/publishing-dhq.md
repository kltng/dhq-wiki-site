# Publishing in DHQ

## Overview

Publishing is a keyword in 49 DHQ articles, but when combined with access (82), the cluster encompasses approximately 131 articles addressing digital scholarly communication, open access, new forms of publication, and the transformation of academic publishing by digital technologies. As a journal *about* digital humanities that is itself *a product of* digital humanities thinking, DHQ occupies a unique position: it is both a subject and an object of its own discourse about publishing.

From its founding, DHQ has been conceived as an experiment in scholarly publishing. The journal is open-access, published under a Creative Commons license, uses detailed XML encoding for all articles, and actively encourages multimedia and interactive submissions. DHQ articles about publishing thus reflect on the journal's own practices as well as on broader transformations in scholarly communication.

## Arguments and Evolution

### DHQ as Publishing Experiment

The founding editorial (DHQ:000007) positions DHQ as deliberately experimental in three ways: (1) open access under Creative Commons licensing; (2) detailed XML encoding to create a "growing research archive that will itself become an object of study"; and (3) encouragement of "experimentation with the forms of scholarly publication," including multimedia and interactive submissions. The editors acknowledge that "the world may not need yet another academic journal, but it does need experiments in how academic journals are published."

### Recognition of Digital Scholarship

Joseph Raben (DHQ:000006) raises a perennial concern: the failure of traditional academic reward structures to recognize digital publications. If digital work does not count toward tenure and promotion, the incentive to produce it is diminished. This article is part of a broader DHQ discourse on the institutional conditions necessary for digital scholarship to flourish.

### The "Done-ness" Problem

David Sewell (DHQ:000039) argues that early celebrations of digital "open-endedness" — particularly Landow and Delany's influential claims about hypertext — created a "culture of the perpetual prototype" that has hampered digital scholarship's entry into peer-reviewed publication. For scholarly publishers, the question of when a digital project is "done enough" to review and publish is both practical and theoretical.

### Global Publishing and Access

Ernesto Priani Saisó's account (DHQ:000014) of digital publishing at UNAM (Mexico) reflects DHQ's attention to global perspectives on scholarly communication, highlighting how digital publishing can both enable and complicate cross-cultural scholarly exchange.
