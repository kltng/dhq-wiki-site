# Collaboration and Interdisciplinarity in DHQ

## Overview

Collaboration (136 articles) and interdisciplinarity (66 articles), combined with project management and related terms, form a cluster of approximately 278 articles — one of the largest thematic areas in DHQ. This cluster addresses the organizational, social, and intellectual dimensions of working across disciplinary boundaries, building large-scale digital projects, and managing the complex teams that DH work requires.

DHQ's coverage reveals that collaboration in DH is not merely a practical necessity but a defining intellectual characteristic of the field. The multi-author articles common in DHQ — some with 10+ co-authors spanning multiple institutions and disciplines — embody the collaborative ethos they describe. From the Suda On Line's collaborative translation of Byzantine scholarship (DHQ:000025) to the Million Book Library's team of classicists, computer scientists, and digital librarians (DHQ:000034), DHQ documents a field that practices what it preaches about crossing boundaries.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Collaboration as Intellectual Practice

DHQ articles consistently argue that DH collaboration differs from traditional humanities solo scholarship. The Suda On Line project (DHQ:000025) exemplifies this: a collaborative translation of a massive Byzantine encyclopedia that would be impossible for any single scholar, requiring distributed expertise across classical philology, digital tools, and project management. Anne Mahoney's account highlights both the possibilities (democratized access to scholarly knowledge, distributed expertise) and the challenges (quality control, sustainability, credit allocation).

### The Team Science Model

The multi-author articles in DHQ's classics cluster (DHQ:000023–000035) exemplify what might be called the "team science" model of DH. Gregory Crane's projects at the Perseus Digital Library routinely bring together classicists, computer scientists, digital librarians, and NLP engineers. These collaborations generate both new knowledge (about classical texts) and new methodological insights (about how to combine computational and humanistic expertise).

### Interdisciplinarity and "Productive Unease"

Julia Flanders (DHQ:000055) frames interdisciplinarity not as comfortable synthesis but as productive friction. The encounter between humanities sensibilities and computational methods generates a "productive unease" that is the source of genuinely new intellectual insights. This perspective suggests that interdisciplinary work in DH is most valuable when it maintains — rather than resolves — the tension between different disciplinary perspectives.

### Project Management and Institutional Challenges

DHQ articles also attend to the practical challenges of managing collaborative DH projects: funding cycles, team turnover, institutional barriers to interdisciplinary work, and the difficulty of evaluating collaborative scholarship within traditional academic structures. Lynne Siemens's work on developing academic capacity in DH (DHQ:000114) addresses these institutional dimensions directly.
