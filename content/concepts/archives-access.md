# Archives, Access, and Digitization in DHQ

## Overview

The cluster of archives (87 articles), access (82), digitization (73), and data curation (70) forms one of the largest thematic areas in DHQ, encompassing approximately 312 articles when combined. This cluster addresses the fundamental DH activity of creating, preserving, and providing access to digital representations of cultural materials. DHQ's coverage reflects the field's deep investment in the practical and theoretical challenges of building digital archives, the politics of access, and the complex relationship between digitization and knowledge production.

DHQ articles consistently demonstrate that digitization is not a neutral act of copying but a transformative process that raises questions about representation, selection, and power. Kenneth Price's influential article "Edition, Project, Database, Archive, Thematic Research Collection: What's in a Name?" (DHQ:000053) exemplifies this concern, interrogating the terminological frameworks through which scholars understand digital scholarly resources.

## Arguments and Evolution

### The Politics of Digitization

DHQ articles consistently frame digitization as a political act. Julia Flanders (DHQ:000055) notes that the "rhetoric of abundance" in digital collections has shifted emphasis from selection to comprehensiveness, making it "now easier, in some contexts, to digitize an entire library collection than to pick through and choose what should be included." This has profound implications for canon formation: "the rare, the lesser-known, the overlooked, the neglected, and the downright excluded are now likely to make their way into digital library collections, even if only by accident." Projects like the Women Writers Project exemplify the deliberate recovery of marginalized materials.

### Genre Confusion and the Nature of Digital Resources

Kenneth Price (DHQ:000053) argues that the proliferation of terms for digital scholarly resources — edition, project, database, archive, thematic research collection — reflects genuine uncertainty about what these resources *are* and how they relate to existing scholarly genres. Price contends that this terminological confusion has practical consequences for how digital work is evaluated, funded, and sustained.

### Completion, Sustainability, and the "Perpetual Prototype"

Susan Brown et al. (DHQ:000040) and David Sewell (DHQ:000039) explore the tension between the open-ended nature of digital projects and the scholarly need for completion. Sewell argues that early theorizing about hypertext — particularly Landow and Delany's celebration of the "incomplete" — contributed to a "culture of the perpetual prototype" (Flanders's phrase) that has hampered digital projects' entry into traditional systems of peer review and academic evaluation.

### Access and Democratization

The "access" keyword in DHQ encompasses both practical access (making materials available online) and critical access (examining who benefits from digitization and who is excluded). DHQ articles attend to digital divides, the politics of metadata, and the ways in which digitization projects can both enable and constrain scholarly engagement with cultural materials.
