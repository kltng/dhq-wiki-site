# Digital Humanities in DHQ

## Overview

The concept of "digital humanities" (DH) is the single most tagged keyword in *Digital Humanities Quarterly*, appearing in 182 articles. Yet DHQ's own coverage reveals a field that resists simple definition. From its inaugural issue in April 2007, DHQ has served as both a mirror and a crucible for debates about what DH *is*: a methodology, a discipline, a community of practice, or something more elusive. The journal's founding editorial by Julia Flanders, Wendell Piez, and Melissa Terras positioned DHQ itself as an experiment — not just another academic journal but a "gathering point for the best digital humanities research" that would simultaneously become "an object of study" in its own right through its detailed XML encoding of articles (DHQ:000007).

The "dh" keyword in DHQ's taxonomy functions as a capacious marker applied to articles that self-consciously reflect on the field's identity, scope, infrastructure, and intellectual trajectory. It overlaps substantially with almost every other keyword in the taxonomy, suggesting that "DH" in DHQ is less a bounded topic than a meta-category — the water in which all other discussions swim.

## Arguments and Evolution

### DH as Identity and Community (2007–2009)

Early DHQ articles are preoccupied with naming and legitimacy. Wendell Piez's editorial "Something Called 'Digital Humanities'" (DHQ:000020) responds to William Deresiewicz's dismissal of DH as mere academic fashion — "something called digital humanities" — by reframing the field as continuous with the deepest traditions of humanistic inquiry. Piez argues that DH is not a subdiscipline but a form of *media consciousness* analogous to what literary scholars have always practiced: "the proper object of Digital Humanities is what one might call 'media consciousness' in a digital age, a particular kind of critical attitude analogous to, and indeed continuous with, a more general media consciousness as applied to cultural production in any nation or period." Piez further compares DH scholars to the humanist scholar-technologists of the Renaissance who designed typefaces, founded publishing houses, and invented the modern critical edition.

### Productive Unease and Critical Engagement (2009–2012)

Julia Flanders's "The Productive Unease of 21st-century Digital Scholarship" (DHQ:000055) argues that the defining characteristic of DH is not technological progressivism but a critical friction between the affordances of digital tools and the habits of humanities thought. Drawing on John Unsworth's characterization of humanities computing as "a practice of representation, a form of modeling," Flanders contends that genuine DH work produces a "productive unease" — "a sense of friction between familiar mental habits and the affordances of the tool" that is "ideally a provocative friction, an irritation that prompts further thought and engagement." Where this unease is absent, she argues, "humanities computing, in the meaningful sense, is also absent."

### Systematization and Conceptual Frameworks (2012–present)

Paul Rosenbloom's "Towards a Conceptual Framework for the Digital Humanities" (DHQ:000127) marks a turn toward more systematic attempts to define the field's structure, proposing that DH can be analyzed through the lens of computing disciplines and their relationships to humanities domains. This reflects a broader trend in DHQ from defensive self-justification toward confident theoretical elaboration.

### Recurring Tensions

Several tensions recur across DHQ's coverage of DH:
- **Big tent vs. definition**: Should DH be inclusive or sharply defined?
- **Tool-building vs. theory**: Is DH primarily about building things or about generating new kinds of knowledge?
- **Ancillary vs. transformative**: Is computing a servant to humanities insight, or does it fundamentally change what humanities scholarship can be?
- **Progress vs. critique**: Is DH characterized by technological optimism or by a critical stance toward its own tools?

## Key Voices

- **Julia Flanders** (Brown University) — Founding editor of DHQ; theorist of digital text representation and scholarly communication; articulated "productive unease" thesis
- **Wendell Piez** (Mulberry Technologies) — Co-founding editor; XML/markup theorist; framed DH as media consciousness
- **Melissa Terras** (UCL) — Co-founding editor; digital humanities infrastructure and image processing
- **Johanna Drucker** (UCLA/UVA) — Frequent DHQ contributor; critical theorist of digital humanities and graphical knowledge
- **John Unsworth** (cited extensively) — Defined humanities computing as knowledge representation
- **Willard McCarty** (KCL) — Philosopher of humanities computing; object of multiple DHQ reviews
