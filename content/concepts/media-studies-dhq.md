# Media Studies in DHQ

## Overview

Media studies is a remarkably broad category in DHQ, encompassing 117 articles directly and combining with cultural criticism (83) and visual art (55) to cover approximately 255 articles. DHQ's media studies coverage ranges from media archaeology and the history of digital media to new media theory, visual culture, electronic literature, gaming, and digital art. The journal has served as a bridge between traditional media studies (film, television, radio) and the computational concerns central to digital humanities.

A distinctive feature of DHQ's media studies coverage is its engagement with *materiality* — the material conditions of digital media production, circulation, and reception. Articles repeatedly interrogate the ontological status of digital objects, the relationship between digital and analog media forms, and the cultural politics of media technologies.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Media Archaeology and the Digital

Steve F. Anderson's "Aporias of the Digital Avant-Garde" (DHQ:000011) examines the paradoxes that arise when experimental media practices are taken up by digital platforms, arguing that the digital simultaneously enables and constrains avant-garde media production. Belinda Barnet's media-archaeological study of the Memex (DHQ:000015) traces how Vannevar Bush's vision of associative information retrieval evolved through subsequent technological developments, positioning the Memex as a "boundary object" between analog and digital media cultures.

### Visual Culture and Digital Representation

Morris Eaves's "Picture Problems" (DHQ:000052) articulates the particular challenges that images pose for digital scholarly editing — challenges that differ fundamentally from those posed by texts. Where textual markup languages like TEI have mature standards and communities, visual materials require different representational strategies. John Walsh's Comic Book Markup Language (DHQ:000117) extends this concern to comics, developing a markup standard that can represent the complex interplay of text, image, sequence, and layout.

### Electronic Literature and New Media Communities

Scott Rettberg's "Communitizing Electronic Literature" (DHQ:000046) argues that electronic literature (e-lit) requires not just new reading practices but new forms of community — shared standards for preservation, curation, and critical discourse. This reflects a broader DHQ concern with how digital media forms generate (or fail to generate) the institutional structures that sustain scholarly engagement.

### Materiality and Digital Ontology

Across DHQ's media studies coverage, a persistent concern is the materiality of digital objects — their status as simultaneously immaterial (code, data) and material (stored on physical media, accessed through hardware). Aden Evens's "Web 2.0 and the Ontology of the Digital" (DHQ:000120) directly addresses this, arguing that digital media resist easy categorization in terms of traditional material/immaterial distinctions.
