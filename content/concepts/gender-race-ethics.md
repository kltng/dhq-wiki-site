# Gender, Race, and Ethics in DHQ

## Overview

The cluster of gender (55 articles), race (50), and ethics (54) encompasses approximately 159 articles that examine how digital humanities intersects with questions of identity, power, representation, and moral responsibility. DHQ has served as an important venue for the "critical DH" movement, which insists that computational methods are not neutral but are shaped by — and in turn shape — the social relations of gender, race, and power.

This coverage ranges from empirical studies that use computational methods to analyze gendered and racialized language patterns, to theoretical critiques of how DH tools and practices may reproduce existing inequalities, to ethical reflections on the responsibilities of digital scholars toward marginalized communities and endangered knowledge systems.

## Arguments and Evolution

### Computational Analysis of Identity

The ARTFL group's studies (DHQ:000042, DHQ:000043) are landmark applications of machine learning to questions of gender and race in literary language. Argamon et al. apply comparative text mining to identify features distinguishing male and female authors, Black and non-Black playwrights, and American and non-American dramatic works. These studies demonstrate the empirical power of computational methods while explicitly acknowledging their limitations: "By framing the classification tasks as binary oppositions (male/female, etc), the possibility arises of stereotypical or 'lowest common denominator' results which may gloss over important critical elements" (DHQ:000043).

### Critical DH and the Ethics of Representation

A parallel strand in DHQ's coverage examines how digital methods and infrastructures may encode biases. Articles on text encoding standards, metadata schemas, and digital archives reveal that decisions about what to digitize, how to describe it, and who gets access are always ethically charged. The encoding of endangered Tibetan texts (DHQ:000004) raises questions about who has the right to digitize and disseminate culturally sensitive materials.

### Gender and the Politics of Digital Scholarship

DHQ's founding editors — Julia Flanders, whose Women Writers Project has been a landmark feminist digital project, and Melissa Terras — have ensured that gender politics are a persistent concern in the journal. Articles on the representation of women writers, the gender dynamics of digital scholarly communities, and the feminist politics of digital editing appear throughout DHQ's run.

### Race, Diaspora, and Digital Recovery

The "race" keyword in DHQ is closely associated with work on African-American and Black diaspora literary traditions. The Black Drama project (DHQ:000043) uses computational methods not just to classify but to illuminate the distinctive linguistic and thematic features of Black dramatic traditions across multiple national contexts.
