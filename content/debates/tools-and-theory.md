# Tools and Theory: Building vs. Thinking in Digital Humanities

## Overview

The tension between tool-building and theory-generating is one of the defining internal debates of digital humanities. Should DH be primarily concerned with building digital tools and infrastructure that serve humanities research, or should it focus on generating new theoretical insights about culture, knowledge, and representation? DHQ has been a key venue for articulating this tension, which is not merely a division of labor but a deeper question about the intellectual identity of the field.

This debate intersects with questions about the "ancillary" status of digital work: is tool-building a service activity that supports "real" humanities scholarship, or is it an intellectual practice in its own right that generates its own forms of knowledge?

## The Debate

### The "Handmaiden" Problem

Julia Flanders (DHQ:000055) articulates the "handmaiden" view — that "technology implements what humanities insight projects as a research trajectory" — in order to argue against it. Flanders contends that the relationship between tools and theory is "potentially more complex": representational technologies like XML, databases, and visualization tools "effect a distancing, a translation which, like any translation or transmediation, provides a view into...the deep discursive structures of the original expression." In other words, building tools *is* doing theory.

### Tools as Embodied Theory

John Bradley (DHQ:000121) extends this argument by showing how annotation tools embody particular theories of annotation. Current tools, oriented toward a "media" model of digital objects, may not adequately support the kinds of annotation humanities scholars actually need. Building better tools requires thinking more carefully about what annotation *is* — a theoretical question. This exemplifies the broader principle that every tool encodes assumptions about its domain.

### The Oulipian Alternative

Mark Wolff's Oulipian framework (DHQ:000005) offers a distinctive position: Oulipian constraints are "better understood as toys with no intended purpose rather than as tools we use with some objective in mind." This reframes the tool/theory binary by suggesting that the most productive digital tools may be those that resist instrumentality — that don't serve a predetermined purpose but instead open new spaces for play, exploration, and discovery.

### Infrastructure as Intellectual Work

David Sewell's account of digital scholarly publishing (DHQ:000039) and the Acadia HyperMedia Centre (DHQ:000016) demonstrate that building DH infrastructure — publishing platforms, research environments, pedagogical tools — is itself a form of intellectual work that requires deep engagement with scholarly practices and theoretical assumptions.

### The "Productive Unease" Criterion

Flanders (DHQ:000055) proposes a criterion for distinguishing genuine DH work from mere technology use: "where that sense of friction is absent — where a digital object sits blandly and unobjectionably before us, putting up no resistance and posing no questions for us — humanities computing, in the meaningful sense, is also absent." This suggests that the value of tool-building lies not in the tools themselves but in the intellectual friction they generate.
