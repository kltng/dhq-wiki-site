# What Counts as Digital Humanities? Debating Field Boundaries

## Overview

The question "what is digital humanities?" has been both a running joke and a genuine intellectual problem since the field acquired its current name around 2004–2005. DHQ has been a primary venue for this debate, publishing 182 articles tagged "dh" that collectively map the field's contested boundaries. The debate plays out across multiple dimensions: is DH a discipline or a methodology? A community or a set of practices? A continuation of humanities computing by other means, or something genuinely new?

## The Debate

### The Big Tent vs. Narrow Definition

One axis of debate concerns the scope of DH. The "big tent" position, articulated in various forms across DHQ, holds that DH should encompass any humanities work that engages with digital technology — from using digital archives to building computational tools. The narrow definition position, drawing on John Unsworth's influential distinction, holds that genuine DH involves a particular kind of intellectual engagement with computation — specifically, using the computer as "a tool for modeling humanities data and our understanding of it," which is "entirely distinct from using the computer when it models the typewriter, or the telephone" (cited in DHQ:000055).

### DH as Continuity vs. Rupture

Wendell Piez (DHQ:000020) argues for continuity, comparing DH scholars to Renaissance humanist-technologists who designed typefaces and invented the critical edition: "Not only do we study digital media and the cultures and cultural impacts of digital media; also we are concerned with designing and making them." In this view, DH is not a break from the humanities tradition but its fullest expression in a digital age. Others argue that computational methods genuinely transform what humanities scholarship can be, creating new objects of study, new methods of analysis, and new forms of argument.

### Technology Servant vs. Technology Master

A recurrent tension in DHQ's coverage is between views of technology as ancillary to humanities insight (the "handmaiden" model, critiqued by Flanders in DHQ:000055) and views that grant technology a more transformative role. Flanders argues that the relationship between digital tools and humanities scholarship is "potentially more complex" than the servant model allows: representational technologies "effect a distancing, a translation which, like any translation or transmediation, provides a view into...the deep discursive structures of the original expression."

### Naming and Legitimacy

Piez (DHQ:000020) notes with characteristic irony that the name "Digital Humanities" replaced "Humanities Computing" as part of a broader dynamic of identity politics: "the rule of identity politics seems to be that for one's old identity to become fashionable, you need a new name for it." The naming question — what to call this field — is inseparable from questions of academic legitimacy, institutional positioning, and intellectual genealogy.
