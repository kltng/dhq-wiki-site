# Access, Openness, and Digital Divides

## Overview

The cluster of access (82 articles), publishing (49), and related concerns constitutes a significant debate within DHQ about the democratization of knowledge, the politics of open access, and the digital divides that persist even — or especially — in a field defined by its engagement with digital technology. DHQ is itself an open-access journal, making this debate both abstract and personal: the journal's own practices are part of the phenomenon its articles analyze.

This debate encompasses multiple dimensions: open access to scholarly publications, access to digital cultural materials, the global reach of DH infrastructure, the digital divides that separate resource-rich and resource-poor institutions, and the question of who benefits from digitization and who is excluded.

## The Debate

### Open Access as Principle and Practice

DHQ's founding editorial (DHQ:000007) makes open access a defining principle: "it is an open-access journal, freely available to the public, and published under a Creative Commons license that permits copying, distribution, and transmission of the work for non-commercial purposes, as long as attribution is made. Copyright remains with the author." This positions DHQ as part of a broader open-access movement that challenges traditional models of scholarly publishing. However, DHQ articles also acknowledge that open access is not free — someone pays for server infrastructure, editorial labor, and technical maintenance.

### The Rhetoric of Abundance

Julia Flanders (DHQ:000055) articulates the "rhetoric of abundance" that characterizes much DH discourse about digital access: the idea that digitization naturally leads to universal availability. Flanders notes that this rhetoric has had genuine positive effects — making "the rare, the lesser-known, the overlooked, the neglected, and the downright excluded" more accessible. But she also implies that this rhetoric can obscure the selective processes that still shape what gets digitized, by whom, and for whom.

### Global Digital Divides

Ernesto Priani Saisó's account of digital publishing at UNAM (DHQ:000014) highlights the global dimension of access: digital scholarly communication is overwhelmingly conducted in English and centered on North American and European institutions. Non-Anglophone scholars face barriers of language, infrastructure, and institutional support that limit their participation in the global DH conversation. DHQ's own coverage, while occasionally addressing these issues, reflects this Anglophone center of gravity.

### Institutional Barriers to Access

Joseph Raben (DHQ:000006) raises a different kind of access question: if digital publications are not recognized by tenure and promotion committees, they are effectively inaccessible to junior scholars who cannot afford to publish in venues that don't count. Institutional recognition is a form of access — access to academic legitimacy, employment, and career advancement.

### The Politics of Digitization

Who decides what gets digitized? Whose cultural heritage is preserved online, and whose is left to molder in inaccessible archives? DHQ articles on archives and cultural heritage consistently raise these questions, arguing that digitization is a political act that reflects and reproduces existing power structures even as it promises to democratize access.

### Access for Whom?

A persistent question across DHQ's coverage is: access for whom? Open-access journals serve scholars with internet connections and institutional support. Digital archives serve researchers who can read the languages and understand the metadata schemas used to describe materials. The "access" keyword in DHQ thus opens onto a much broader set of questions about equity, inclusion, and the distribution of knowledge in a digital age.
