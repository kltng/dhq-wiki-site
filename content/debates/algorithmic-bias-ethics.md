# Algorithmic Bias, Ethics, and Justice in Digital Humanities

## Overview

The intersection of ethics (54 articles), race (50), gender (55), and algorithmic methods constitutes a growing debate within DHQ about the moral and political dimensions of computational humanities work. This debate encompasses multiple concerns: the biases encoded in algorithms and training data, the representational politics of digitization and metadata, the ethics of working with indigenous and endangered knowledge systems, and the broader question of whether DH tools and practices reproduce or challenge existing structures of power and inequality.

DHQ has been a venue for both empirical demonstrations of these issues (e.g., the ARTFL group's reflexive analysis of their own classification methods) and theoretical reflections on the ethical responsibilities of digital scholars.

## The Debate

### The Bias of Binary Classification

The ARTFL group's Black Drama study (DHQ:000043) provides one of DHQ's most explicit engagements with algorithmic bias. The authors acknowledge that "by framing the classification tasks as binary oppositions (male/female, etc), the possibility arises of stereotypical or 'lowest common denominator' results which may gloss over important critical elements, and may also reflect the experimental design." This admission is significant: it demonstrates that leading practitioners of computational text analysis are aware that their methods may encode and reproduce the very categories they seek to analyze. The question is what to do about it.

### Normalization and Its Discontents

The same study notes that "the most successful classifications for author and character genders were accomplished by normalizing the data in various ways" but that doing so "creates a kind of distance from the text as originally composed, which may limit the interpretive utility of classification tools." This raises a fundamental ethical question: is it acceptable to transform texts — stripping them of their original complexity — in order to make them amenable to computational analysis?

### Indigenous Knowledge and Digital Ethics

Linda Patrik's article on Tibetan texts (DHQ:000004) raises questions about the ethics of digitizing endangered and culturally sensitive materials. Who has the right to digitize? Who controls access to digitized materials? How can digital preservation respect the cultural protocols that govern access to indigenous knowledge? These questions have become increasingly urgent as digitization projects proliferate.

### Algorithmic Agency and Responsibility

John Johnston's "Webbots and Machinic Agency" (DHQ:000126) addresses a more abstract dimension of the ethics debate: if algorithms exhibit forms of agency — making autonomous decisions, generating unexpected outputs — who is responsible for their actions? This question connects DH ethics to broader debates about AI accountability and the politics of automation.

### Critical DH and Social Justice

A growing strand in DHQ's coverage argues that DH has a particular responsibility to address issues of social justice — both because computational methods can reveal patterns of inequality that are invisible to traditional methods, and because DH tools and infrastructures may reproduce existing inequalities if they are not designed with justice in mind. This "critical DH" position insists that ethics is not an add-on to DH practice but integral to it.
