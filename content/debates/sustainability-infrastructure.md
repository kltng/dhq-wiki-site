# Sustainability and Infrastructure: The Challenge of Permanence in DH

## Overview

The sustainability of digital humanities projects — their ability to persist, remain accessible, and continue to function over time — is one of the most practically urgent and theoretically interesting debates in DHQ. With infrastructure (93 articles) and data curation (70 articles) as major keywords, DHQ documents a field that is acutely aware of the fragility of its own creations. Digital projects depend on technologies, platforms, and funding streams that may not endure; they face the perpetual challenge of technological obsolescence, institutional instability, and the difficulty of maintaining complex technical systems beyond their initial development phase.

This debate intersects with the "perpetual prototype" problem (Flanders's phrase, cited in DHQ:000039): the tendency of DH projects to remain in a state of permanent development, never reaching the kind of completion that would enable peer review, institutional adoption, and long-term preservation.

## The Debate

### The "Perpetual Prototype" Problem

David Sewell (DHQ:000039) traces the "perpetual prototype" to early hypertext theory's celebration of open-endedness: Landow and Delany's influential claim that "hypertext materials are by definition open-ended, expandable, and incomplete." Sewell argues that this theoretical conflation of the "open" with the "unfinished" has had practical consequences, delaying digital scholarship's entry into traditional peer review and publication systems. Julia Flanders's memorable phrase "a culture of the perpetual prototype" captures the institutional dynamic: projects remain in perpetual development because funding rewards innovation over maintenance.

### The "Done-ness" Question

Matthew Kirschenbaum's editorial "Done: Finishing Projects in the Digital Humanities" (DHQ:000037) frames the sustainability problem in terms of completion: when, if ever, is a digital project "done"? Susan Brown et al. (DHQ:000040) argue that DH projects are characterized by a fundamental tension between "projection and completion" — between the desire to expand, improve, and extend, and the need to stabilize, preserve, and publish. This tension is not merely practical but philosophical, reflecting different understandings of what digital scholarly objects *are*.

### Cyclical Sustainability

William Kretzschmar (DHQ:000038) proposes a cyclical model of sustainability: "every end is a new beginning." Drawing on his experience with the Linguistic Atlas Project, Kretzschmar argues that large-scale DH projects should be understood not as linear endeavors with a clear endpoint but as cyclical processes that renew themselves through successive phases of development, maintenance, and reinvention.

### Infrastructure at Scale

The Scaife Digital Library vision (DHQ:000035) represents the most ambitious approach to sustainability: building institutional infrastructure on a scale that can support long-term preservation and access. Blackwell and Crane argue that classical philology requires — and can serve as a model for — comprehensive digital infrastructure that integrates digitization, encoding, NLP, linked data, and user-facing tools into a sustainable ecosystem.

### Data Curation and Preservation

The "data curation" keyword (70 articles) reflects DHQ's attention to the specific challenges of preserving digital data: format migration, metadata standards, persistent identifiers, repository infrastructure, and the institutional structures needed to support long-term data stewardship.
